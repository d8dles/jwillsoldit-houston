Buying your first home in the Houston area comes down to getting the order right: what a lender checks first, what to save for beyond the down payment, and which loan and assistance program actually fit your numbers. This guide walks through that order, step by step.

## Start with what a lender checks first

Before a lender gives you a real number, they look at two things: your credit and your debt-to-income ratio, or DTI. Start by pulling your full credit report and fixing any errors you find. [AnnualCreditReport.com](https://www.annualcreditreport.com/) is the only site required by federal law to give you free reports from all three credit bureaus, and you're entitled to one free report from each bureau every week. Skip any other site that asks for a credit card number for a "free" report. Then add up your monthly debts: car payments, student loans, credit cards, plus the new mortgage payment. Divide that total by your gross monthly income. Most lenders want that number at or below about 43 percent, though the exact limit depends on the loan program and the lender you use. If you open a new credit card, finance a car, or change jobs in the months before you apply, both numbers can move the wrong way. Try to keep your finances steady all the way through closing, not just until you get pre-approved.

## The down payment isn't the only number to save for

Down payment minimums vary a lot. VA and USDA loans can go all the way to zero down. Many conventional and FHA loans start at 3 to 3.5 percent down. The traditional 20 percent down payment avoids monthly mortgage insurance. On top of the down payment, plan for closing costs, which usually run 2 to 5 percent of the purchase price. That covers the appraisal, title work, lender fees, and prepaid taxes and insurance. Buyers who save only for the down payment often get caught off guard at the closing table. The Texas assistance programs below can help cover both.

## A pre-approval beats a pre-qualification

A pre-qualification is a quick estimate based on what you tell the lender. A pre-approval is different. The lender actually verifies your income, assets, and credit, then gives you a letter showing a conditional commitment. That letter is what sellers and agents take seriously when you make an offer. Getting pre-approved before you start touring homes also gives you a realistic price range, so you don't waste time looking at houses you can't afford. Call two or three lenders before you pick one. Fees and rates vary enough between lenders to matter over 30 years.

## The four main loan types

A conventional loan can go as low as about 3 percent down for a qualifying first-time buyer. It usually wants a credit score around 620, and it requires private mortgage insurance if you put down less than 20 percent, though you can drop that insurance later. An FHA loan, insured through HUD, allows 3.5 percent down with a credit score of 580 or higher, or 10 percent down for scores between 500 and 579. It carries its own mortgage insurance for most of the loan's life. A VA loan is available to eligible veterans, active-duty service members, and some surviving spouses. It usually requires no down payment and no monthly mortgage insurance, though it does have a funding fee you can roll into the loan. A USDA loan, backed by Rural Development, also allows zero down payment in eligible areas, and more of the Houston area qualifies than most people expect. It does come with income limits. None of these loans is automatically the best choice. Each one fits a different mix of credit, savings, service history, and location.

## Houston and Texas both offer real help for first-time buyers

The City of Houston Housing and Community Development Department runs a Homebuyer Assistance Program that offers up to $75,000 in forgivable, no-interest help toward a down payment and closing costs. It's for income-qualified buyers purchasing inside city limits, and it works as a lien that gets forgiven after five years in the home. The Texas Homebuyer Program, run by the Texas Department of Housing and Community Affairs, offers My First Texas Home: a 30-year mortgage paired with down payment and closing cost help through a network of approved lenders. The Texas State Affordable Housing Corporation runs its own loan and down payment assistance programs, including some built specifically for the Houston area. Every one of these programs has its own income limits, purchase price limits, and required homebuyer education course, and the available funding changes every year. [HUD keeps a directory of approved housing counselors](https://www.hud.gov/counseling) who teach that course and can also answer general questions for free. Start here, but confirm the current details before you count on any of it.

## The offer, the inspection, and the appraisal check different things

Your offer includes the price, earnest money, any contingencies, and a proposed closing date. Earnest money is a good-faith deposit, usually 1 to 2 percent of the price, and it gets credited toward closing. Think of it as a deposit, not a fee. A home inspection checks the physical condition of the house and typically costs $300 to $500, though it can run higher for a larger home. You pay for it, and your lender doesn't require it. An appraisal is different. Your lender orders it independently to check whether the price matches the home's value, and it protects the loan, not your negotiating position. It usually costs $300 to $600, and that fee typically falls on you too, spelled out on your loan estimate. If you're thinking about waiving the inspection to make your offer more competitive, know what you're giving up before you agree to it.

## Closing day, and the mistakes that cost buyers the most

A final walkthrough, usually the day of or the day before closing, confirms the home is in the condition your contract describes. At closing, you sign the loan documents, pay any remaining closing costs, and the deed transfers to you. The most expensive mistakes are also the easiest to avoid: changing jobs or financing a big purchase between pre-approval and closing, skipping the inspection because you're in a hurry, only getting one lender's quote, and trusting wire instructions without calling the title company to confirm them by phone. That last one matters more than it sounds. Wire fraud targeting real estate closings is a real, ongoing problem, and a phone call is the entire fix.
